# Title Proposal

**Slug:** the-tyranny-of-optimality

**Why this matters:** The entire self-improvement industry is built on a hidden assumption: that there is a *best* way to do things, and your job is to find it and execute it. But a growing body of research in decision science and behavioral economics suggests that "good enough" isn't a consolation prize — it's often the *optimal* strategy. The tension: the more you optimize, the worse your outcomes get.

**Core thesis:** Maximizing is a cognitive error. Satisficing — choosing the first option that meets your threshold — produces better decisions, less regret, and more happiness than exhaustive optimization.

**Key sources:**
- Article: [The Tyranny of Choice](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2004/04/choice) (Barry Schwartz, APA) — More options increase anxiety and decrease satisfaction; the "maximizer" personality type correlates with depression and regret.
- Study: [Maximizing versus satisficing: Happiness is a matter of choice](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2006-21193-006) (Schwartz et al., *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2002) — Maximizers score lower on happiness, optimism, and self-esteem; higher on regret and perfectionism.
- Study: [The cost of thinking too much](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1803308115) (Bhatia & Mullett, *PNAS*, 2018) — More deliberation time does not improve decision quality beyond a threshold; it increases error on complex choices.
- Book: *Decisive* by Chip & Dan Heath — "Shrink the change" and "narrow your options" — the authors show that good decision-makers deliberately limit their choice set.
- Thread: [The case for quitting](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=40000000) (Hacker News, 2024) — Engineers on sunk-cost fallacy and why "sticking with it" is often worse than cutting losses.

**Possible angles:**
1. **The optimization trap in skill-building** — People who try to find the "perfect" learning method (best course, best app, best routine) spend more time researching than practicing. Deliberate practice research (Ericsson) shows that *volume of reps* beats *method elegance* — yet the industry sells method.
2. **The counterintuitive take: "good enough" is a feature, not a bug** — Satisficing isn't laziness; it's a *computational strategy*. Your brain has finite processing power. Every calorie spent on choosing is a calorie not spent on doing. The person who picks a mediocre gym and goes is fitter than the person who spends 6 months finding the perfect one.

**Target audience hook:** You've been told that "you get what you settle for." That's wrong. The research says the opposite: the people who settle *early* and *often* are the ones who get more done, feel better, and regret less. If you've ever spent 3 hours comparing productivity apps instead of working, this article is for you.

**Related past articles:** None on this specific angle. Closest: "The Discipline Myth" (if exists) — but this is distinct: it's not about willpower, it's about *decision strategy*.
