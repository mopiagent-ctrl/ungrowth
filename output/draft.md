# The Anti-Resilience Manifesto

> Resilience isn't a skill you're failing to learn — it's a defense mechanism you've learned too well.

## The Problem

Somewhere along the way, we turned resilience into a moral virtue. Bounce back. Adapt. Keep moving. The language is martial, aspirational, clean. It makes for good LinkedIn posts and better corporate training modules. And it's quietly destroying your capacity for actual growth.

I spent a decade in medicine watching people die, heal, and everything in between. The ones who "bounced back" fastest from devastating diagnoses were often the ones who returned six months later with the same lifestyle, the same stress patterns, the same arterial plaque rebuilding itself cell by cell. They hadn't transformed. They'd just gotten better at pretending the heart attack was an interruption rather than information.

The research backs this up in ways that should make us deeply uncomfortable with the entire resilience industry.

## What The Data Says

George Bonanno's work at Columbia has been foundational to how we understand trauma response — and it contains a finding that resilience advocates rarely mention. In his 2011 study on bereavement and potential trauma, Bonanno found that individuals classified as "resilient" (those showing minimal disruption in daily functioning after loss) demonstrated significantly *lower* scores on post-traumatic growth measures.

Let that land. The people who appeared most functional were the ones who grew least.

A 2009 paper by Mancini and Bonanno dug deeper into the predictors of this pattern. High resilience scores correlated with avoidant attachment styles and lower emotional granularity — the ability to distinguish between nuanced emotional states. In other words, the "resilient" weren't processing better. They were processing less. They'd developed sophisticated ways to not feel what was happening to them, and the research was mistaking numbness for strength.

Peter Levine's somatic experiencing framework, outlined in *Waking the Tiger*, offers a biological explanation. When an organism faces threat, the nervous system mobilizes for fight, flight, or freeze. If the response cycle completes — if the animal shakes off the excess activation, discharges the energy — the trauma resolves. But if the cycle gets interrupted, if the organism "bounces back" to normal functioning before the physiological response completes, that undischarged activation gets stored as chronic tension. The body stays braced for a threat that's already passed.

Resilience, as commonly practiced, is that interruption. It's the premature return to baseline that leaves the deeper work undone.

## The Counterintuitive Part

Post-traumatic growth isn't hiding behind the trauma, waiting for you to get past it. It's *inside* the trauma, accessible only if you stay there long enough to find it.

This is where Taleb's distinction between resilience and anti-fragility becomes useful, even if he was writing about financial systems. A resilient system absorbs shocks and returns to its original state. An anti-fragile system *requires* volatility to improve — it gets stronger through exposure to stressors, not protection from them.

Bones are anti-fragile. Apply controlled stress, they densify. Remove all stress, they atrophy. Your psychological growth works the same way. Resilience is the cast you put on the bone — it protects, but it also prevents the loading that stimulates remodeling. Stay in the cast too long, and the bone weakens.

The people who grow most from failure, heartbreak, and loss aren't the ones who "bounce back" in six weeks. They're the ones who stay in the wreckage for six months, who let the old identity fully dissolve before building a new one, who experience what the research literature calls "sustained disruption of core beliefs" — and don't rush to resolve it.

## So What

The practical implications are uncomfortable because they require doing less, not more.

First, stop treating emotional distress as a problem to solve. When you feel shattered after a breakup or a layoff, that's not a malfunction. That's your meaning-making machinery doing exactly what it evolved to do — forcing you to confront the gap between what you believed and what actually happened. The impulse to "bounce back" is often just the ego's attempt to avoid that confrontation.

Second, reject any framework — corporate, therapeutic, or self-help — that frames recovery as an individual responsibility without addressing the environment. The Atlantic's 2023 piece on resilience training documented what anyone who's sat through a corporate wellness seminar already suspects: these programs function primarily as responsibility-shifting mechanisms. If you're burned out, the problem isn't the 60-hour weeks or the toxic manager. The problem is your insufficient resilience. Here's a webinar.

This is gaslighting with a PowerPoint deck.

What actually works: psychological safety in teams, adequate recovery time, structural changes that reduce chronic stressors rather than training individuals to endure them. But those things cost money and require leaders to change their behavior. Resilience training costs $50 per employee and lets everyone keep doing exactly what they were doing.

Third, develop what the emotion researchers call "emotional granularity." The Mancini and Bonanno finding — that resilient individuals show lower emotional granularity — suggests that precise feeling is a growth mechanism. When you can distinguish between disappointment and betrayal, between sadness and grief, between frustration and helplessness, you're doing the cognitive work that transforms experience into insight. "I feel bad" is a dead end. "I feel the specific shame of having ignored warning signs I pretended not to see" is a doorway.

## The Bottom Line

Resilience promises you can go through hell and come out the same person. The people who actually grow come out different — and that requires staying in the fire long enough to let the old self burn.

Stop bouncing back. Stay broken for a while. It's the only way to rebuild something worth having.
