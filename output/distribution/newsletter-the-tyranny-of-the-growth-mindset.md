**Preview:** We spent a decade telling people they can do anything — and forgot to teach them when they shouldn't.

---

**Body:**

I've been thinking a lot about quitting lately.

Not the dramatic, door-slamming kind. The quiet, strategic kind. The kind where you look at something you've poured months or years into and realize: *this isn't working, and no amount of effort is going to fix it.*

Here's the thing — I spent years believing that quitting was weakness. That persistence was the only virtue that mattered. That if I just tried harder, believed harder, pushed harder, everything would eventually work out.

Then I started reading the actual research.

Carol Dweck's growth mindset work? Real, but the effect sizes were always modest. The big *Nature* study in 2019 — 12,000 students — found the intervention only helped kids performing below the 50th percentile. For high achievers, it actually *hurt* their performance.

Meanwhile, the goal disengagement research from Concordia University shows something almost heretical: people who can abandon unattainable goals have lower cortisol, better sleep, fewer depressive symptoms, better physical health. The ones who kept pushing toward impossible goals? They got sicker.

**Key insight from this week:**

Persistence is often just loss aversion wearing a motivational costume. The skill that actually separates people who thrive from people who burn out isn't the ability to persist — it's the ability to *discriminate*. To know which goals deserve your stubbornness and which deserve your goodbye.

I've seen this play out everywhere. The patient staying in a career that's destroying their health because "growth requires discomfort." The founder pouring another year into a product nobody wants because "winners never quit." The student switching majors not because they found a better fit, but because they've internalized that struggle equals virtue.

Adam Grant said it best in his exchange with Angela Duckworth: grit only predicts success when the goal is worth pursuing. Grit aimed at the wrong target is just expensive stubbornness.

So here's what I'm trying to practice now: setting kill criteria before I start things. Separating my identity from my projects. Trusting my boredom as information. And asking myself the question that cuts through all the motivational noise — *if I were starting fresh today, knowing what I know now, would I choose this goal again?*

If the answer is no, that's not a reason to keep going. That's a reason to leave.

Quitting isn't the opposite of growth. It's the mature form of it.
