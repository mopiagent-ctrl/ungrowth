# LinkedIn Post

I used to believe quitting was a character flaw. Then I spent a decade watching what it does to people who refuse to do it.

The growth mindset was supposed to liberate us from the belief that ability is fixed. Instead, it became a moral cudgel. Quit your job? Fixed mindset. Walk away from a failing project? You didn't believe hard enough.

I've seen the damage this does. The patient staying in a career that's destroying their health because "growth requires discomfort." The founder pouring another year into a product nobody wants because "persistence separates winners from losers."

Here's what the research actually says.

The 2019 *Nature* study — 12,000 students, a national experiment — found growth mindset interventions only helped students performing below the 50th percentile. For high performers, the effect was zero. In some contexts, it was negative. Telling capable people "you can grow with effort" undermined their calibration.

Meanwhile, the goal disengagement research from Concordia University shows people who can abandon unattainable goals have lower cortisol, better sleep, fewer depressive symptoms, and better physical health. The ones who kept pushing toward impossible goals got sicker.

Persistence, it turns out, is often just loss aversion wearing a motivational costume.

The skill that separates people who thrive from people who burn out isn't persistence. It's discrimination — knowing which goals deserve your effort and which deserve abandonment.

The most effective people I know quit fast on the wrong things and slow on the right ones. They treat quitting as a strategic tool, not a moral failure.

The growth mindset was supposed to free us from the tyranny of fixed ability. Instead, it created a new one: the obligation to persist regardless of whether persistence makes sense.

The real skill is knowing when to grow and when to go.

What's your experience with knowing when to walk away?
