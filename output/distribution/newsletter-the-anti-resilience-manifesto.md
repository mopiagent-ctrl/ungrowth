Hey friend,

I have a confession: I used to wear resilience like a badge of honor. The tougher things got, the prouder I was of my ability to "handle it." Toxic job? I'll adapt. Cold relationship? I'll grow. Project turning into a grind? I'll push through.

I thought I was getting stronger. Turns out, I was just getting better at suffering.

This week's article hit me hard because it names something I've felt but never had words for: the difference between genuine growth and efficient endurance. Between stretching yourself and dismantling yourself.

**Key insight from this week:**

Resilience isn't a character virtue — it's a context-dependent tool. Used on temporary obstacles, it's invaluable. Used on situations that are actively harming you, it becomes a way of nailing yourself to a burning house. The data is clear: people who are "highly resilient" tend to stay longer in toxic workplaces, abusive relationships, and environments that are slowly breaking them down. Not because they're stronger — because they're better at rationalizing damage.

I've been thinking about this in the context of my own life. As someone who's spent years optimizing everything from code to health to daily habits, I realize I've been applying that same optimization mindset to endurance. *How can I get better at tolerating this?* instead of *Should I be tolerating this at all?*

The article offers three questions I've started asking myself — and they're uncomfortable in the best way:

1. Is this situation improving, or am I just getting better at tolerating it?
2. Would I advise someone I love to stay in this?
3. What would I do if I knew leaving wouldn't make me a "quitter"?

The answer to that last one? Quitting a dead end isn't failure. It's intelligence.

So here's my question for you (and I'd genuinely love to hear): **What's one situation in your life right now where you might be confusing endurance with growth?**
