---

Hey,

I spent years thinking resilience was the goal. Bounce back faster. Absorb more. Don't let it show.

Then I started noticing something uncomfortable. The people I knew who prided themselves on being resilient — myself included — weren't actually growing. We were just getting better at staying the same.

I remember sitting with a friend who'd just left her third toxic workplace in five years. She was proud of how quickly she'd bounced back each time. New job within weeks. No visible damage. But she'd never stopped to ask why she kept ending up in those environments. She was too busy being resilient to learn.

That's the trap this week's article is about.

**Key insight from this week:**

The data shows that people who recover fastest from trauma often return to the exact same configuration that got them into trouble in the first place. The "resilient" 15% don't show the most growth — it's the people who struggle, who let the break reorganize them, who end up genuinely different. Resilience isn't a skill you're failing to learn. It's a trap you're succeeding in falling into.

I've been thinking about this a lot in the context of my own work. As a developer, I know that the best systems fail fast and fail loud — they surface the exact line of code that broke and refuse to keep running in a degraded state. We call it strategic brittleness. But somehow when it comes to our own lives, we treat any failure as a bug rather than a feature.

The article introduces a concept I haven't been able to stop thinking about: *intentional breakability*. Designing your life so that when something breaks, it breaks cleanly and informatively. Not shattering into a thousand pieces, but breaking in a way that reveals exactly what needs to change.

I've started asking myself a different question after setbacks. Not "how do I get back to normal?" but "what if normal was part of the problem?"

What would change if you asked yourself that too?
