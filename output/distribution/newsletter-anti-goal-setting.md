Subject: I stopped setting goals. Here’s what happened.

Preview: The advice you’ve heard since childhood—set clear goals, visualize success, commit publicly—is not just useless. For the things that matter, it’s actively harmful.

---

I spent most of my twenties setting goals I didn’t achieve. Sound familiar?

For years, I blamed myself. Not disciplined enough. Not focused enough. The usual self-flagellation. But then I noticed something strange: the areas where I made the most progress—writing, clinical decision-making, relationships—were precisely the areas where I had *no specific goals at all*. I just showed up and did the work.

Meanwhile, the goals I set with perfect specificity—“publish 12 papers this year,” “build a 30-minute daily meditation habit”—reliably collapsed within weeks.

This wasn’t a motivation problem. It was a tool problem. I was using a hammer to paint a watercolor.

**Key insight from this week:**

Goal-setting works great for simple, repeatable tasks—assembly lines, data entry, hitting a sales quota. But for the domains where your life actually happens—career, relationships, creative work, health—the path is unknown and constantly shifting. When you fixate on a specific outcome, your brain optimizes for that outcome at the expense of everything else. You stop noticing opportunities that don’t fit the script. You cut corners when you’re close but falling short. Research from Harvard, Northwestern, and UPenn calls this “Goals Gone Wild”—and it’s been documented for over a decade.

**Why this matters right now:**

I’ve been thinking about this because I’m in the middle of a career transition. The old me would have set a BHAG (Big Hairy Audacious Goal) and tried to force the path. Instead, I’m experimenting with systems: showing up daily, paying attention to what the work teaches me, and adjusting. It’s harder than setting a goal—no dopamine hit from a finish line—but it works in the places where goals don’t.

The practical shift looks like this: Instead of “I will write a book this year,” commit to “I will write for 90 minutes every morning before I check my phone.” Instead of “I will be a more patient parent,” commit to “I will pause for three breaths before responding to frustration.”

These aren’t watered-down goals. They’re harder. But they’re the only thing that actually moves the needle.
