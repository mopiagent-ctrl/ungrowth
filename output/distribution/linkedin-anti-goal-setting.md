**LinkedIn Post**

I used to believe that if I wasn’t chasing a specific goal, I was failing.

Then I spent a decade watching my most meaningful progress happen in areas where I had zero goals at all.

I wrote papers, improved my clinical judgment, and deepened relationships—not because I set SMART targets, but because I showed up and did the work. Meanwhile, every perfectly specified goal I set—"publish 12 papers," "meditate 30 minutes daily"—collapsed within weeks.

This isn’t a motivation problem. It’s a tool problem.

A 2009 paper in *Academy of Management Perspectives* called "Goals Gone Wild" documented the dark side: narrowed focus, increased cheating, reduced intrinsic motivation, and inhibited learning. When you fixate on an outcome, your brain optimizes for that outcome at the expense of everything else. You stop noticing opportunities that don’t fit the script.

Even the classic Locke and Latham review—the one goal evangelists love—found that goals work best on simple, well-defined tasks. Assembly lines. Data entry. The moment you introduce complexity or creativity, the effect reverses.

The domains where your life actually happens—career, relationships, creative work, health—are precisely the domains where the path is unknown.

The deeper trap: every goal is a bet on a future self. The person who sets the goal in January is making predictions about what the person in December will want. Those two people are not the same. You either achieve the goal and feel empty, or abandon it and feel like a failure.

The alternative isn’t aimlessness. It’s navigation. Show up. Do the work. Pay attention to what the work is teaching you. Adjust. Repeat.

Stop asking what you want to achieve. Start asking what you’re willing to do every day, regardless of outcome.

What’s one area where you’ve made more progress without a specific goal than with one?

**Comment 2 (Reply to predicted FAQ: "But don't I need goals to measure progress?"):**
Great question. The key distinction is between *outcome goals* (lose 20 pounds) and *process commitments* (train consistently). You can measure progress on the process without being locked into a specific future self. The data shows that in complex domains, process-focused people outperform goal-focused people over time—because they adapt when conditions change.

**Comment 3 (Reply to predicted counterargument: "What about elite athletes? They set goals."):**
Fair point. But elite athletes use goals as *training targets*, not identity anchors. A runner aiming for a 4:00 mile doesn't define their worth by it—they use it to calibrate their training. The problem is when the goal becomes the measure of your value. That's when the narrowing and cheating effects kick in. Systems for the process, goals for the calibration.
