Hey,

I spent years trying to force myself into optimism. I’d close my eyes, picture the finished project, the glowing feedback, the sense of relief. And you know what? It felt great for about five minutes. Then I’d open my eyes, look at the blinking cursor, and feel the exact same dread I felt before I started.

The worst part wasn't the lack of progress. It was the guilt. I thought I wasn't "believing hard enough."

Then I stumbled onto this research about "defensive pessimists," and it genuinely rewired how I approach everything—both in my clinical work and in my own coding projects.

**Key insight from this week:**
The people who succeed aren't the ones who believe everything will work out. They’re the ones who asked what would happen if it didn’t—and then acted on the answer. Psychologists found that "defensive pessimists" perform *just as well* as optimists. But here’s the kicker: when they forced these people to think positively, their performance *dropped*. The anxiety wasn't a bug; it was a planning mechanism. It’s data.

This hit home for me because I’m a natural worrier. I used to see that as a weakness. But the article explains that a true pessimist gives up, while a defensive pessimist uses the fear to build a checklist.

I tried the "pre-mortem" technique this week on a project I’ve been procrastinating on. I wrote down "It's six months from now, this failed." Then I listed every specific reason why. It took 15 minutes. The anxiety didn't disappear, but it turned into a to-do list. I finally started.

If you’re tired of feeling like a fraud for not being positive enough, or if you have a project you’re avoiding because you’re scared it won’t be good enough—this one’s for you.
