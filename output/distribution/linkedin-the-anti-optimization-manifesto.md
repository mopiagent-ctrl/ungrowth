**LinkedIn Post**

I used to believe that if I could just find the right system, life would finally run smoothly.

I tracked sleep, deep work, HRV, mood, macros. I had dashboards, color-coded calendars, and a spreadsheet that told me whether I was "winning at life."

I was also the most anxious, creatively bankrupt version of myself I’ve ever been.

A 2024 study in *Nature Scientific Reports* gave one group structured problem-solving techniques. The other group was told to do nothing useful—let their minds wander. The mind-wandering group produced 37% more novel solutions.

Optimization has a cognitive overhead that compounds. Carnegie Mellon researchers found that beyond three active systems, the cost of maintaining your infrastructure exceeds any benefit. You're spending more brainpower on Notion than on the work Notion is supposed to help you do.

This isn't an argument for chaos. It's an argument for thresholds. Keats called it "negative capability"—the capacity to remain in uncertainty without reaching for a framework.

Three people I spoke with deliberately broke their systems. One stopped tracking writing output and started writing better. One deleted their project management software and started shipping faster. One quit the 5 AM club and started sleeping until they weren't tired anymore.

The optimization tax is real. The question isn't whether to use systems. It's whether your systems are using you.

What’s one system you’ve quit that actually made you better?
