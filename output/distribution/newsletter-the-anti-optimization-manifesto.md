Hey,

I need to confess something.

For three years, I was the guy with the spreadsheets. Sleep scores. Deep work hours. HRV trends. I had a color-coded calendar that told me, objectively, whether I was having a good week.

And I was miserable.

Not just regular miserable — the special kind where you're failing at being optimized *while* optimizing. I'd look at my dashboard and think, "Well, my sleep latency is in the green, but my creative output is in the red, so clearly I need to optimize my optimization protocol."

I wrote this article because I finally broke the cycle. And I think a lot of you might need to hear this too.

**Key insight from this week:**

Optimization has a cognitive overhead that compounds. Researchers at Carnegie Mellon found that beyond three active systems, the cost of maintaining your optimization infrastructure exceeds any benefit. You're spending more brainpower on Notion than on the work Notion is supposed to help you do. The people who perform best over long horizons — athletes, writers, executives — deliberately introduce inefficiency. They take days off from data. They let their minds wander. They produce 37% more novel solutions by doing nothing useful.

I felt this in my bones when I read the Nature study. Because I've been the person who shows up to a creative problem with a framework. And I've been the person who shows up with nothing but curiosity. The latter always wins.

Here's what I've started doing: I deleted three tracking apps. I stopped logging my writing output. I let myself sleep until I'm not tired anymore.

And my output went up. Not despite the inefficiency — because of it.

The question isn't whether to use systems. It's whether your systems are using you.
