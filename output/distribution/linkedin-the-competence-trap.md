# LinkedIn Post

I spent 12 years becoming excellent at something. Then I realized it was quietly making me obsolete.

That's the competence trap. The skills that got you here are not the skills that will get you there — and the longer you've been rewarded for them, the more they're costing you.

Most career advice assumes growth means getting better at what you already do. More skill. More discipline. More optimization. But the research points somewhere darker.

The Peter Principle isn't just about over-promotion. A brilliant engineer who becomes a manager doesn't just need new skills. They need to *stop* using the skills that earned them the promotion. The instinct to solve problems directly — the very thing that made them valuable — now undermines their team.

The Curse of Knowledge makes it worse. Once you know something deeply, you lose the ability to remember what it felt like not to know it. Experts overestimate what novices can understand, which makes them worse teachers, worse communicators, and worse at recognizing when their own mental models are outdated.

Then there's Escalation of Commitment. The more time and identity you've poured into a competence, the harder it is to walk away — even when the evidence says you should. You don't just lose the skill. You lose the story you tell yourself about who you are.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the highest-leverage growth move available to you right now is probably not acquiring a new skill. It's shedding an old one.

I call this the anti-portfolio. Just as investors track their mistakes to avoid repeating them, you should track your competencies to identify which ones have become liabilities. What you stop being good at matters more than what you become good at.

Every six months, ask yourself:
1. What am I currently rewarded for?
2. What's the trend line — growing, stable, or shrinking?
3. What would I need to stop being good at to make room for what's next?

This is terrifying because competence is identity. You're not just someone who *does* backend engineering. You're a backend engineer. Letting go feels like dying.

But the alternative is worse. You can cling to a competence that's depreciating, doubling down as the returns diminish, until you're the person still optimizing for a world that no longer exists.

What you built was a bridge, not a destination. You crossed it. Now you're on the other side — and the bridge is burning behind you whether you like it or not.

What skill are you holding onto that might be costing you more than it's giving you?

**Comment 2:**
A lot of people ask: "Does this mean I should abandon my specialization entirely?" No. The point isn't to become a generalist who's mediocre at everything. It's to recognize that deep specialization is a fragile strategy — it works until the environment shifts. The people who thrive long-term aren't the ones who accumulate the most expertise. They're the ones willing to let go of expertise that's stopped serving them, before the evidence forces their hand.

**Comment 3:**
Someone raised a good counterpoint: "What if I'm still getting paid well for this skill?" That's exactly the trap. Income is a lagging indicator. The market rewards you for what was valuable yesterday. By the time compensation catches up to the shift, you've already lost years. The question isn't "Am I still being rewarded?" It's "Is the trend line still pointing up?" Be honest with yourself. Look at the data, not your feelings.
