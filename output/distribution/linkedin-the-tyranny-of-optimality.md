# LinkedIn Post

I spent three hours last week choosing a note-taking app. Not using one. Choosing one.

By the time I made a decision, I was too exhausted to write anything down.

This is called maximizing — the compulsion to examine every option before committing. Psychologist Barry Schwartz found that people who do this are measurably less happy, less optimistic, and more depressed than those who don't.

Here's the kicker: they don't even get better outcomes.

Research shows that beyond a certain threshold, more deliberation makes decisions worse, not better. Your brain has a finite budget of decision-making energy. Every calorie spent choosing is a calorie not spent doing.

The person who picks a mediocre gym and goes three times a week is fitter than the person who spends six months researching the perfect program and never starts.

"Good enough" isn't a consolation prize. It's a computational strategy.

Three shifts that helped me:

1. Set a threshold before you start looking. Not "the best app" but "one that syncs and doesn't crash." Take the first option that clears the bar.

2. Put a time limit on decisions. The cost of a wrong choice is almost always recoverable. The cost of paralysis is not.

3. Recognize that regret is a mindset, not a consequence. The wondering is a choice.

Optimization is a tax on action. The people who get the most done aren't the ones who make the best decisions — they're the ones who decide fast enough to learn from them.

**Comment 2:**
The question I always get: "But what about big decisions — career moves, relationships, major purchases?"

Fair pushback. The research suggests the same principle applies, just with a higher threshold. The mistake isn't setting a high bar. It's refusing to define the bar before you start looking. If you can't articulate what "good enough" looks like before you begin, you'll never know when to stop.

**Comment 3:**
Someone will say: "But I've made great decisions by exhaustively researching."

That may be true. But survivorship bias is real — you remember the times thoroughness paid off and forget the dozens of hours spent on choices that didn't matter. The question isn't whether research ever helps. It's whether the marginal benefit justifies the marginal cost. For most decisions, it doesn't.
