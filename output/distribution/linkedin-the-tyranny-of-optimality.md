# LinkedIn Post

I spent three hours last Tuesday choosing a note-taking app.

Not organizing notes. Not writing anything useful. Just comparing features, reading Reddit threads, and building a mental matrix of pros and cons across six applications. By the time I made a decision, I had no energy left to take a single note.

This isn't a quirky personal anecdote. It's the logical endpoint of a culture that's convinced us optimization is a virtue — that the gap between where we are and where we want to be is just a matter of finding the right system.

The premise is wrong. Not just philosophically — empirically.

In 2002, psychologist Barry Schwartz published a study that should have ended this obsession before it took hold. He identified two types of decision-makers: **maximizers** (who seek the absolute best option) and **satisficers** (who set a "good enough" threshold and move on).

The findings were brutal. Maximizers scored lower on happiness, optimism, and self-esteem. Higher on regret, depression, and rumination. And here's what most people miss: they didn't just feel worse — they *achieved less*. The fear of choosing wrong leads to choosing late, choosing nothing, or switching too often to build momentum.

The satisficer's advantage is temporal. By the time the maximizer has chosen the perfect workout program, the satisficer has been training for six weeks. The quality gap between their programs — even if it exists — is dwarfed by the gap in accumulated effort.

The best system is the one you'll use. The optimal system, almost by definition, is too fragile to survive contact with reality.

Three shifts that helped me:

**Set hard time limits on decisions.** 30 minutes for a tool. 24 hours for a career move. The constraint forces you to identify what actually matters versus what you're analyzing to feel productive.

**Define "good enough" before you start looking.** Three criteria. First option that clears all three wins. You've already decided what you're missing doesn't matter.

**Stop treating regret as information.** Feeling bad about a choice doesn't mean you made the wrong one. It's often just the emotional residue of having had options at all.

Growth doesn't come from finding the perfect system. It comes from the unsexy, unoptimized work of doing the thing, learning from the mess, and doing it again.

What's your experience with the optimization trap?

This is the most common pushback I get. The data actually shows the opposite — high performers are excellent at *starting things and abandoning them*. The people who accumulate the most practice hours in any domain aren't the ones with the most elegant systems. They're the ones who keep showing up, often with methods that are messy and decidedly suboptimal by any theoretical standard. Excellence is a compounding function, not a selection function.

**Comment 3:**
*Reply to predicted counterargument — "Some decisions genuinely require exhaustive research"*

Agreed — I'm not arguing for recklessness. There's a difference between a high-stakes decision (medical treatment, major financial commitments) and the 90% of decisions where the downside of being wrong is modest and recoverable. The problem is we treat everything like the former. Schwartz's work suggests the threshold for "good enough" should scale with the stakes — and most of us set it far too high for the decisions that actually move the needle.
