# LinkedIn Post

I used to believe "good enough" was a failure of character. Then I read the research.

For two decades, I chased the optimal life. Cold plunge before sunrise. Zone 2 cardio. Gratitude journal. Protein logged in three apps.

I was doing everything right. And I was more exhausted than ever.

Not physically. Existentially.

Here's what I learned: the framework is broken. Not me.

In 2002, Barry Schwartz's research showed that maximizers — people who always seek the optimal choice — score significantly lower on life satisfaction than satisficers. They experience more regret. Higher rates of depression.

Then came the most damning finding: maximizers often make *objectively better* choices. Better jobs. Better outcomes.

They just feel worse about all of it.

You can be winning by every metric that matters and still feel like you're losing.

The self-improvement industry has run a twenty-year experiment on our psychology. The hypothesis: better tools, better data, better systems = better humans.

The results are in. The experiment failed.

Every productivity app raises the bar for what counts as an acceptable day. Every tracker creates a new metric you can fail. Every "optimal routine" article implies your current morning is deficient.

The people who buy in most deeply aren't the success stories. They're the casualties.

Satisficers aren't lazy. They're strategic. When you accept "good enough" on things that don't matter, you free bandwidth for things that do.

The skill worth developing isn't optimization. It's knowing *when* to optimize.

Your career. Your health. Your closest relationships. Those deserve your full attention.

What you eat for breakfast? Which app you use? Whether your workout is optimal or merely good? These aren't high-stakes choices. Treating them like they are doesn't make them matter more. It just makes you tired.

I'm not arguing for nihilism. I'm arguing for discrimination.

Set a standard, not a target. Timebox your decisions. Audit your optimization stack. Cultivate the skill of being done.

The happiest people aren't the ones who make the best choices. They're the ones who've learned to stop choosing.

What's one thing you've stopped optimizing — and how did it change things?

**Comment 2 (reply to predicted FAQ — "But doesn't this justify mediocrity?"):**
This is the most common pushback I get. The answer is no — this isn't an argument for mediocrity. It's an argument for *discrimination*. The point isn't to stop caring. It's to care selectively. Optimize the decisions that compound over decades (career, health, relationships). Satisficing on the rest isn't settling for less — it's freeing up the cognitive bandwidth to actually excel at what matters.

**Comment 3 (reply to counterargument — "I thrive on optimization"):**
Fair point — and there's real variance here. Some people genuinely enjoy the process of optimization itself. The research doesn't say maximizers never succeed; it says they pay a measurable cost in satisfaction and regret. If you're optimizing and feel great, you're probably the exception. But worth asking: are you optimizing because it serves you, or because the industry convinced you that anything less is failure? That distinction matters more than the routine itself.
