# LinkedIn Post

I used to believe that if I could just optimize every variable, I'd finally feel whole.

Better sleep. Better output. Better macros. Better "readiness scores."

Then I realized something uncomfortable: the tools that promise to make you better are the same tools that ensure you never feel good enough.

A 2020 study in Computers in Human Behavior found it's not tracking itself that harms us. It's the mode. When tracking is used for awareness, effects are neutral to positive. When it shifts to evaluation — grading yourself against a standard — anxiety spikes and life satisfaction drops.

The mechanism is brutal. Your sleep becomes a "score." Your walk becomes 6,200 steps toward a goal someone else decided was optimal. You stop experiencing your life and start auditing it.

Byung-Chul Han called this the achievement society. You become your own overseer. And unlike an external boss, you can never clock out.

The counterintuitive truth: the problem isn't that you're failing at optimization. It's that optimization, pursued seriously, is a form of self-erasure. Every metric you track sends a low-grade signal: *you are not acceptable as you are.*

The alternative isn't passivity. It's strategic neglect. Some domains — relationships, creativity, rest — degrade under measurement. "Good enough" isn't a compromise. It's often the optimal strategy for long-term satisfaction.

I'm not saying throw away your tools. I'm saying audit them. For every metric you track, ask: awareness or evaluation? Who set the standard?

The goal was never to become a perfectly optimized machine. It was to build a life you don't need to escape from.

What's your experience with self-tracking — helpful tool or quiet trap?

I hear you, and I want to be precise: the research doesn't say tracking is bad. It says the *mode* matters. The question is whether the same tool that caught the problem has started grading you in ways that erode your sense of self. That's the line worth watching.
