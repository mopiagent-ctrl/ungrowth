Hey,

I hit this wall last month. Hard.

I was doing all the right things—tracking my writing output, reviewing my weekly metrics, optimizing my morning routine. I was *improving*. And yet, the quality of my work felt flat. The ideas felt recycled. The joy was gone.

I did what any rational self-improver would do: I tried harder. I added a second review session. I cut my sleep by 30 minutes to journal more. I bought a new app to track my focus.

And it got worse.

That’s when I stumbled down a rabbit hole that led me to a paper on chess grandmasters. The researchers asked them to try harder on a memory task. Their performance *dropped*. The conscious effort to improve disrupted the automatic processing that made them experts.

It was the most validating thing I’ve read in years.

**Key insight from this week:**
Self-improvement follows a U-shaped curve. Deliberate practice is the rocket fuel for the first 80% of your potential. But once you cross that threshold, the same engine becomes an anchor. Your brain needs periods of non-directed engagement to consolidate learning and escape the ruts that constant optimization creates. The better you get, the more your improvement machinery actively interferes with the automatic processing that high-level performance requires.

I’ve been thinking about this a lot because I’m a recovering optimizer. I have a spreadsheet for my sleep, a graph for my mood, and a calendar block for my "creative flow." It worked—until it didn’t. The data shows that deliberate practice explains only about 12% of the variance in expert performance. Twelve percent. The rest is a mix of luck, breadth of experience, and the ability to let go.

So I’m trying something uncomfortable this month: deliberate non-improvement. I’m writing without tracking word count. I’m lifting without logging reps. I’m letting myself be bad at things again.

It feels like heresy. But I think it’s the only way through.

What’s one metric you’re tracking right now that might actually be holding you back?
