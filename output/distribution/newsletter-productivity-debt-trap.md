Hey,

I spent last Sunday doing something I’m not proud of: I re-organized my Notion dashboard.

The thing is, I haven't actually *used* that dashboard in about three months. But there I was, at 10 AM, dragging databases around and color-coding tags for a system that serves no purpose other than making me feel like a "productive person."

It hit me while I was doing it: I wasn't working. I was paying interest.

We talk a lot about "optimization" here, but this week I wrote about the dark side of that coin. It’s called the Productivity Debt Trap, and I think it’s the silent killer of actual progress.

**Key insight from this week:**

Every time you adopt a new app or methodology without retiring an old one, you take out a loan against your future cognitive capacity. You might feel organized in the moment, but you’re actually signing up for a lifetime of maintenance payments. The average person is running 7-12 active systems at once. That’s not a productivity stack; that’s a second job that doesn’t pay.

I’ve been guilty of this more than anyone. As a doctor, I’m wired to optimize. As a coder, I love a good system. But I had to admit that I was spending more energy maintaining my "growth infrastructure" than actually growing. The red badges on my habit tracker weren't helping me; they were debt collectors demanding payment.

The counterintuitive fix isn't finding the *perfect* app. It’s strategic neglect. It’s having the guts to delete 40 hours of work because keeping it would cost you 400 more.

I go into the specific "bankruptcy" steps in the article, but the TL;DR is this: you don't need more tools. You need the courage to have fewer.

Let me ask you: What is the one system or app you are keeping alive out of guilt, not because it actually serves you?
