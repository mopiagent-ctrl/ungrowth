# LinkedIn Post

I used to believe the perfect system would set me free.

The habit tracker pinging at 5:47 AM. The color-coded calendar. The sleep score at 92. The meditation streak at 214 days.

By every metric I set for myself, I was winning.

And yet the anxiety was worse than when I started. I caught myself thinking about my "deep work block" during my daughter's birthday party. Somewhere between the gratitude journal and the quarterly goal review, I stopped being a person and became a dashboard.

Historian Jerry Z. Muller calls this "metric fixation" — and his research shows it doesn't just fail to improve things. It actively distorts them. Surgeons game mortality stats by refusing high-risk patients. Teachers teach to the test. We hit our KPIs while the real work burns.

The psychology is even more uncomfortable. A 2018 study in *Psychological Science* found that external monitoring undermines intrinsic motivation. When you track your reading, you read for the streak. When you track your workouts, you train for the green checkmark. Something essential dies.

Here's what I've come to believe: optimization is a bet that tomorrow looks like today. It works beautifully for known problems with known solutions. But the most consequential moments of a life — the career pivot, the creative breakthrough, the relationship crisis — never arrive as items on your task list. They arrive as disruptions to it.

The capacity to handle disruption isn't built through efficiency. It's built through slack. Google's 20% time gave us Gmail. 3M's 15% rule gave us Post-it Notes. Breakthrough innovation emerges from the margins — the very margins optimization tries to eliminate.

So I've started protecting my inefficiency. Blocking time with no purpose. Taking the long way home. Sitting in the park with no agenda.

The research is clear: those "wasted" hours are where insight, adaptation, and genuine satisfaction actually live.

What's your experience with the optimization trap?

**Comment 2:**
A few people have asked: "Does this mean I should stop tracking everything?" No — the point isn't anti-discipline. It's anti-totalizing discipline. If you want to train for a marathon, a structured plan works. The problem starts when you apply optimization to things that are destroyed by it — creativity, judgment, love. The question isn't "should I have systems?" It's "what are my systems in service of?"

**Comment 3:**
Someone raised a fair counterpoint: "Isn't this just an excuse for laziness?" I'd push back gently. There's a difference between slack and avoidance. Slack is intentional unstructured time — the walk without a podcast, the conversation without an agenda. Avoidance is running from responsibility. The research on incubation periods shows the brain solves hard problems *during* unstructured time, not instead of working on them. The slack isn't the absence of work. It's part of the work.
