**LinkedIn Post**

The people who adapt best to life aren't the ones who bounce back fastest. They're the ones who know when staying down is the smarter move.

For twenty years, we’ve been sold resilience as a moral imperative. Get knocked down, get back up. Adapt. Pivot. Stay strong. It sounds noble. It feels actionable. And it’s half the story.

The other half is darker. A 2023 piece in *Harvard Business Review* showed that chronic resilience correlates with emotional suppression and delayed recovery. The people who are "best" at resilience—who keep going, who don't complain—are often the ones quietly accumulating the most damage. They’re not processing. They’re storing.

I’ve seen this in medicine. The patient who tells me they’re "fine" after a devastating diagnosis worries me more than the one crying in the exam room. The crier is doing the work. The resilient one is building up debt.

Here’s the counterintuitive part: pain is not a malfunction. It’s a signal. Physical pain tells you to pull your hand off the stove. Emotional pain tells you something is wrong with your environment, your relationships, your boundaries. Resilience that bypasses processing is like silencing a smoke alarm because the noise is annoying.

The alternative isn’t fragility. It’s *deliberate non-resilience*—the practice of staying in discomfort long enough for it to teach you something. Sometimes the strongest thing you can do is fall apart, pay full attention to the pieces, and rebuild something that doesn’t need to be so goddamn resilient in the first place.

What’s your experience with the pressure to "bounce back"?

**Comment 2 (Reply to predicted FAQ: "So should I just give up when things get hard?"):**
Not at all. The point is to distinguish between *adaptive* resilience (acute crisis, a deadline that can't move, a child who needs you steady) and *chronic* resilience (using endurance to avoid processing). The first is a tool. The second is a debt. The question isn't "should I push through?" but "what am I protecting myself from by pushing through?"

**Comment 3 (Reply to predicted counterargument: "Resilience saved my life"):**
I hear this, and I don't want to dismiss anyone's lived experience. Resilience *can* be lifesaving in the moment. The problem is when the moment becomes a lifestyle. If resilience got you through a crisis, that's a win. But if you're still using the same coping mechanism five years later, it's worth asking: what are you not letting yourself feel? The goal isn't to stop being resilient. It's to stop *needing* to be.
