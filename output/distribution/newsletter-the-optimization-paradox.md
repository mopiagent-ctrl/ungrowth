**Body:**

Hey friend,

I need to confess something. I've been that person with the color-coded calendar and the 214-day meditation streak. The one who checks their sleep score before checking on their kids. The one who catches themselves mentally planning their "deep work block" during a birthday party.

If that hits a little close to home, this week's article is for you.

I wrote this one after a particularly brutal week where I realized I'd become a dashboard. Every metric was green. Every habit was on track. And I felt more hollow than I had in years. The system I'd built to serve me had quietly become my master.

**Key insight from this week:**

Optimization is a bet that tomorrow looks like today. It works beautifully for known problems with known solutions — progressive overload for lifting, scheduling for reading, daily practice for languages. But the most valuable human capacities — creativity, adaptability, judgment, love — don't just resist optimization. They're actively destroyed by it. The research is uncomfortable: external tracking undermines intrinsic motivation, and "wasted" idle time is where your brain actually solves hard problems.

I've been sitting with this tension as someone who's spent years building systems — both as a doctor and as someone who genuinely loves optimization. The answer isn't to burn your planner. It's to protect your inefficiency. Block time with no purpose. Take the long way home. Have lunch without a networking goal.

The doorway to your best thinking is usually found in the spaces you didn't schedule.

So here's my question for you: what's one thing you've been tracking that you suspect might be making you smaller instead of better?

I'd genuinely love to hear your answer. Hit reply and tell me.
