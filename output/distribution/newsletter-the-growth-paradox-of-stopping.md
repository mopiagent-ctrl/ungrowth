Subject: Stop grinding — here’s what actually makes you better
Subject: Why your plateau is telling you to do less

Preview: The most reliable way to get better at something is to walk away from it — but only if you do it on purpose.

---

I spent most of my career believing a lie. If I was stuck, I wasn’t working hard enough. If I was plateauing, I needed more hours. More effort. More grit.

Turns out, that belief isn’t just wrong. It’s the exact inverse of the truth.

As a medical student, I’d study for twelve hours straight and retain almost nothing. But when I’d review a concept for twenty minutes, ignore it for three days, then test myself — the knowledge stuck. I thought I was the anomaly. I was the rule.

**Key insight from this week:**
You don’t get better while you’re doing the thing. You get better while you’re not doing it. Measurable skill improvements occur exclusively during sleep and rest periods *after* practice — not during the practice itself. The forgetting phase is functional: when you partially forget something and reconstruct it, the reconstruction is more durable than the original encoding. Growth happens in the gap.

I know how uncomfortable this feels. I’ve built an identity around grinding, around showing up every day, around the idea that more effort equals more results. But the data is clear: elite performers practice *less* total time than intermediates. They do shorter, more intense sessions with longer recovery periods. The difference isn’t volume. It’s density and spacing.

Here’s the part that really got me: when you plateau and double down — adding hours, cutting breaks, pushing through fatigue — you don’t escape the plateau. You plateau harder. The fatigue compounds, your session quality collapses, and the recovery window (the actual engine of growth) gets squeezed to nothing. Persistence without strategic stopping isn’t grit. It’s stubbornness.

So I’ve started doing something that feels deeply wrong: I schedule my gaps. I study something, then walk away for two days — not as a failure of discipline, but as a deliberate part of the protocol. When I come back, I test myself before reviewing. The struggle to retrieve is the mechanism.

The self-improvement industry sold us a linear model of growth because it’s simple to sell. The reality is biological, not mechanical: you grow in the spaces between effort, and those spaces need to be as deliberate as the effort itself.

Stop treating rest like a concession to weakness. It’s the part where the work actually takes.
