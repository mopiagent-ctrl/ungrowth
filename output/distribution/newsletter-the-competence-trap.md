---

**Preview:** The skills that got you here aren't the skills that will get you there — and the longer you've been rewarded for them, the more they're quietly costing you.

---

**Email Body:**

Hey friend,

I've been thinking a lot about this lately, because I see it everywhere — in my own work, in patients, in founders I talk to.

You spend a decade getting really, *really* good at something. It pays off. You're respected, well-compensated, confident. Then one day you realize the problems are changing faster than your solutions. The tools you mastered are being replaced. The questions you're an expert at answering aren't the questions that matter anymore.

Here's the kicker: you're not failing. That's the trap. You're succeeding at something that's quietly becoming obsolete.

**Key insight from this week:**

Competence is a depreciating asset. The more you invest in a skill, the harder it is to abandon — not because it's still valuable, but because it's fused with your identity. You don't just *do* backend engineering. You *are* a backend engineer. Letting go feels like dying.

The research backs this up. The Peter Principle isn't just about over-promotion — it's about how the skills that earned you one level actively disqualify you from the next. The Curse of Knowledge makes experts worse at recognizing when their mental models are outdated. And escalation of commitment means the more time you've poured into a competence, the harder it is to walk away.

The uncomfortable truth: **the highest-leverage growth move available to you right now is probably not acquiring a new skill. It's shedding an old one.**

I call this the anti-portfolio. Just like investors track their mistakes, you should track your competencies to identify which ones have become liabilities.

I've been doing this audit myself — asking what I'm rewarded for, what the trend line looks like, and what I'd need to stop being good at to make room for what's next. It's uncomfortable. But it beats being the person still optimizing for a world that no longer exists.

The skill that made you valuable in your 30s might be the thing that makes you irrelevant in your 40s. Starting over doesn't mean wasting what you built. It means recognizing that what you built was a bridge, not a destination.

What skill are you holding onto that might be costing you more than it's giving?
