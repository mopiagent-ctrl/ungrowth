**LinkedIn Post**

I used to think visualization was the secret. Close your eyes, see the win, and the universe falls in line.

Then I read the research on "defensive pessimism" and realized I had it backwards.

A 1986 study by Norem and Cantor found that people who imagine everything going wrong perform *just as well* as optimists. Not worse. The anxiety they generate gets converted into preparation. They study harder because they're convinced they'll fail.

But here's the kicker: when researchers forced these people to think positively, their performance *dropped*. The affirmations didn't help. They removed the coping mechanism.

The twist is that defensive pessimism isn't actually pessimism. A true pessimist gives up. A defensive pessimist says, "This will fail, so I need to figure out why and fix it before it happens."

The negative expectation is a tool, not a worldview.

This is why Gary Klein's "pre-mortem" works so well. Before a project starts, you imagine it has already failed—spectacularly—and work backward to explain why. It surfaces risks that standard planning misses because it gives people permission to voice doubts.

Positive visualization rewards you for dreaming. Defensive pessimism rewards you for preparing. Only one of those changes outcomes.

The people who succeed aren't the ones who believe everything will work out. They're the ones who asked what would happen if it didn't—and then acted on the answer.

What's your experience with pre-mortems or worst-case scenario planning?

**Comment 3 (Reply to counterargument: "Optimism is what keeps teams motivated"):**
I hear this a lot, and I think there's a middle path. The research doesn't say optimism is useless—it says forced optimism *on the wrong personality type* backfires. The best teams I've seen use a "realistic optimism" approach: believe the outcome is possible, but pre-mortem the path to get there. You keep the motivation, but you add the preparation.
